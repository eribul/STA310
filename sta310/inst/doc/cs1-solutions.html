1. Time-to-event data and censoring

1.

  1. The time origin is the date of diagnosis of metastatic breast cancer. In the data only observed_time is stored, but it is measured from diagnosis.

  2. The time scale is time since diagnosis, measured in months (values such as 40.8 and 13.6 are months; the maximum is 71.2 months = about 6 years).

  3. The event of interest is death (death from any cause, as the indicator is simply status).

  4. status = 0 means the patient was right-censored: she was still alive at the end of follow-up, so we only know that her true survival time is longer than observed_time.

  5. Typical reasons: (i) administrative censoring — the patient was still alive when the study period ended / at data cut-off; (ii) loss to follow-up — the patient moved, transferred to another clinic or withdrew. In this synthetic cohort all censoring is administrative, but in real data both occur.

3.

  1. False. The patient contributes follow-up time up to 10 months and is then removed from the risk set. She is not at risk at 12 months. This is exactly how censoring enters the Kaplan–Meier estimator: censored subjects reduce the size of the risk set at later event times, but no event is counted for them.

  2. False. Informative censoring can bias the estimate in either direction. If sicker patients drop out (e.g. because they become too unwell to attend), their future deaths are missing and survival looks better than it is (upward bias). If patients who are doing well leave the study, survival looks worse (downward bias). The sign depends on how censoring is related to prognosis.

  3. False. The hazard is an instantaneous rate, not a probability. In the lecture \(h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} P(t \le T < t + \Delta t \mid T \ge t)/\Delta t\), so \(h(t)\) can take any value from 0 to \(\infty\) and is not bounded by 1. A probability of dying in a short interval would be approximately \(h(t)\,\Delta t\).

  4. False. Because survival times are right-skewed (a few long survivors), the median (and other percentiles) is preferred. The mean is influenced by the extreme tail and is often poorly estimated unless follow-up is complete.

  5. True. Left-censoring means that the true event time is smaller than the observed time — for example, a patient who is first examined for a condition that had already started earlier. Interval censoring means that we only know that the event happened somewhere between two examination times. The MBC cohort has neither: here all censoring is right-censoring.

4.

Two things are lost in logistic regression:

  • Time. Logistic regression only distinguishes “died” from “not died at the end of follow-up”. It does not use when the death occurred, so a patient who dies after 2 months and one who dies after 6 years contribute equally. Survival analysis uses the full follow-up time.
  • Censoring. Patients who are censored are neither events nor properly “non-events”: we know only that they survived until their censoring time. Logistic regression would have to call them survivors, which treats incomplete follow-up as if it were complete, and this overestimates survival (see Question 8). Survival methods use the follow-up time of censored patients up to the point of censoring.

Moreover, survival time is positive and right-skewed, so ordinary linear regression of the time on the covariates is inappropriate too. The Cox model instead models the hazard and estimates hazard ratios, which is the natural effect measure for time-to-event data.

2. The survivor function and the Kaplan–Meier estimator

5.

  • Median survival is 13.3 months (95 % CI 12.3–15.1). This is the smallest event time at which the estimated survivor function has dropped to 0.5 or below: half of the patients have died within about 13 months of diagnosis.
  • \(\hat S(12) = 0.542\) (95 % CI 0.510–0.577). An estimated 54 % of patients survive at least 12 months (1 year).
  • \(\hat S(24) = 0.303\) (95 % CI 0.273–0.337). An estimated 30 % survive at least 24 months (2 years).

6.

  1. The number at risk just before the time point is 626 at 6 months, 463 at 12 months and 207 at 24 months.

  2. The curve stays flat at a censoring time. The Kaplan–Meier estimate only has steps (“drops”) at event times, where the factor \((1 - d_j/n_j) < 1\). At a censored observation \(d_j = 0\), so the factor is \(1\) and the estimate is unchanged; the only effect of censoring is that it reduces the risk set for later event times (and thereby increases the size of later steps).

  3. The risk set shrinks for two reasons: patients die (events) and patients are censored. Between 6 and 24 months, for example, many patients both die and reach the end of their follow-up, so the number at risk falls from 626 to 207. Censoring removes people from the denominator without producing a step in the curve.

7.

The curve falls steeply during the first 12–24 months — the risk of death is highest shortly after the diagnosis of metastatic disease — and then flattens out. The horizontal line at \(S = 0.5\) crosses the curve at the median, 13.3 months. After about 60 months the curve is almost flat: only a small subgroup of patients is still under observation, and few further deaths are observed among them.

The naive estimate is \(1 - 573/850 = 0.326\), compared with the Kaplan–Meier estimate \(\hat S(24) = 0.303\). The naive value is biased upwards: 71 patients were censored before 24 months and the formula silently counts them as if they had survived all 24 months, even though we do not know that. Kaplan–Meier removes them from the risk set at their censoring time instead.

The bias is modest here because only 17 % of the patients are censored. In a study with heavy censoring (e.g. short follow-up or many drop-outs) the naive estimate could be badly wrong, which is exactly why censoring-aware methods are needed.

3. Hazard and cumulative hazard

10.

The HR-negative curve rises faster and stays above the HR-positive curve, so HR-negative patients accumulate risk (hazard) more quickly — consistent with their worse survival in Question 9. At 24 months the cumulative hazard is roughly \(-\log(0.168) \approx 1.8\) for HR-negative and \(-\log(0.342) \approx 1.07\) for HR-positive patients.

The slope of the cumulative hazard curve at a given time is the instantaneous hazard \(h(t)\). A roughly straight cumulative hazard curve therefore corresponds to a roughly constant hazard (exponential-type survival); a steepening curve means the hazard is increasing over time.

11.

At 12 months the Nelson–Aalen estimate is 0.610 and \(-\log \hat S(12) = 0.612\); at 24 months they are 1.189 and 1.194. \(H(t) = -\log S(t)\) holds for the true functions in continuous time. Nelson–Aalen sums the increments \(d_j/n_j\), whereas the Kaplan–Meier approach multiplies factors \((1 - d_j/n_j)\) and then takes the log; the difference is of second order in \(d_j/n_j\) and is small when the risk set is large (i.e. for small \(t\), large \(n\)). This matches the lecture’s remark that the two estimators give very similar results, especially for small \(t\) with large \(n\).

4. The log-rank test

12.

In the HR-negative group there were 176 observed deaths against 119.0 expected under the null hypothesis; in the HR-positive group there were 527 observed against 584.0 expected. HR-negative patients die more often than expected if the groups were the same, and HR-positive patients less often. The test statistic is \(\chi^2 = 33.5\) on 1 degree of freedom, p = 7 ^{-9}.

Conclusion: there is strong evidence that survival differs between the hormone-receptor groups, with worse survival for HR-negative patients. The log-rank test tells us that the groups differ; it does not by itself quantify the size of the difference — that is the job of the Cox model in Question 16.

  1. The null hypothesis is that all four groups have the same survivor function, \(H_0: S_0(t) = S_1(t) = S_2(t) = S_3(t)\) for all \(t\).

  2. \(\chi^2 = 84\) on 3 degrees of freedom, p < 2 ^{-16}.

  3. This is an omnibus (overall) test. A significant result says that at least one group differs from the others, but not which groups differ, nor who differs from whom. Nor does it quantify the size of the differences — for that you need pairwise comparisons (with a multiplicity adjustment) or a regression model with the ECOG groups as a categorical covariate, which also adjusts for other variables.

Bonus (weighted tests).

The Wilcoxon-type test, in R available as the Peto–Peto modification of the Gehan–Wilcoxon test through survdiff(..., rho = 1), weights each death by \(\hat S(t)^{\rho}\) with \(\rho > 0\). With \(\rho = 1\) late events are down-weighted relative to the log-rank test (\(\rho = 0\)), so early differences count more.

event_times <- sort(unique(mbc$observed_time[mbc$status == 1]))

observed <- expected <- variance <- 0
for (t in event_times) {
  n <- sum(mbc$observed_time >= t) # all at risk at t
  n1 <- sum(mbc$observed_time >= t & mbc$hormone_receptor == "HR-negative") # at risk, HR-neg
  n2 <- n - n1 # at risk, HR-pos
  d <- sum(mbc$observed_time == t & mbc$status == 1) # deaths at t
  d1 <- sum(
    mbc$observed_time == t &
      mbc$status == 1 &
      mbc$hormone_receptor == "HR-negative"
  ) # deaths at t, HR-neg

  observed <- observed + d1
  expected <- expected + d * n1 / n
  if (n > 1) {
    variance <- variance + d * (n - d) * n1 * n2 / (n^2 * (n - 1))
  }
}

c(
  observed = observed,
  expected = round(expected, 2),
  o_minus_e_sq_over_e = round((observed - expected)^2 / expected, 2),
  o_minus_e_sq_over_v = round((observed - expected)^2 / variance, 2)
)
#>            observed            expected o_minus_e_sq_over_e o_minus_e_sq_over_v 
#>              176.00              118.98               27.33               33.51

The loop gives \(O = 176\) and \(E = 118.98\), matching the survdiff() table (176 and 119.0). The \((O-E)^2/E\) component is 27.33 and \((O-E)^2/V\) is 33.51, which is the test statistic \(\chi^2 = 33.5\) reported by survdiff() (for two groups \((O-E)^2/V\) is the correct statistic; the \((O-E)^2/E\) column is only a descriptive component).

15.

Use a stratified log-rank test, which performs the comparison within each stratum of ECOG performance status and then combines the strata. It adjusts for ECOG without assuming proportional hazards across ECOG levels (though it still assumes proportional hazards between the hormone-receptor groups within strata).

survdiff(
  Surv(observed_time, status) ~ hormone_receptor + strata(ecog_ps),
  data = mbc
)

5. Cox proportional hazards regression

16.

  1. HR (HR-positive vs HR-negative) = 0.604, 95 % CI 0.509–0.718, p = 9.7 ^{-9}.

  2. At any point in time, HR-positive patients have about 40 % lower hazard of death than HR-negative patients (0.60 times the hazard), i.e. they die at about 60 % of the rate of HR-negative patients.

  3. The reference level is HR-negative, so the HR for HR-negative versus HR-positive is \(1/0.604 = 1.66\): HR-negative patients have about a 66 % higher hazard of death. Note that this is not the same as saying that they are “66 % more likely to die” within a given time.

  4. The p-value (9.7 × 10⁻⁹) is very close to the log-rank p-value (7 × 10⁻⁹). This is not a coincidence: the score test of the Cox model with a single binary covariate is essentially the log-rank test. The Cox model adds an effect estimate (the hazard ratio), which the log-rank test does not provide.

18.

Bonus (variable selection).

Stepwise selection uses the same data twice: first to choose the variables and then to estimate and test their effects. The reported p-values and confidence intervals then behave as if the model had been specified in advance, so they are too optimistic (the true uncertainty is larger). The selected model is also unstable — small changes in the data can change which variables enter — and it ignores subject-matter knowledge in favour of mechanical p-value thresholds.

A better approach is to pre-specify the covariates from clinical knowledge (as in Question 17), or to use a method that accounts for the selection, such as penalised regression (the lasso for Cox models, Collett Section 3.7) combined with validation. Automatic selection is best used as an exploratory tool, not as the final analysis.

  1. The odds ratio is 0.366 for HR-positive vs HR-negative (95 % CI 0.204–0.614), i.e. 2.73 for HR-negative vs HR-positive. The corresponding hazard ratio was 1.66. The odds ratio is clearly larger.

  2. The two measures answer different questions. The odds ratio compares the odds of being dead at the end of follow-up — a cumulative, time-averaged quantity that depends on how long the study ran. The hazard ratio compares the instantaneous rate of death at any given time among those still alive. For a common outcome (here 83 % of patients died) the odds ratio moves away from 1 much faster than the hazard ratio, so it is not a good approximation of the relative risk or hazard. Logistic regression also ignores when patients died and treats censored patients as survivors.

  3. The statement has several problems:

  • “2.5 times more likely” is a relative-risk interpretation. A hazard ratio is a ratio of rates, not of probabilities; it does not mean 2.5 times the probability of dying.
  • The number 2.5 does not correspond to any estimate here (the OR is 2.73, the HR is 1.66), so it is unclear what it refers to.
  • There is no time frame: “more likely to die” by when? By 1 year, 2 years, or ever?
  • If the number came from the logistic model it also ignores censoring, so it is biased.

A correct version would be, for example: “After diagnosis, HR-negative patients had a 66 % higher hazard of death at any point in time (HR 1.66, 95 % CI 1.42–1.96).”

6. Checking the proportional hazards assumption

20.

The HR-negative curve remains consistently above the HR-positive curve, indicating poorer survival among HR-negative patients. The curves are close together initially, but the vertical distance increases during the first 10–15 months. Thereafter, the curves appear approximately parallel, although some variation in their separation remains. The confidence intervals widen towards the end of follow-up, particularly for the HR-negative group, where fewer patients remain at risk. Overall, the plot suggests some variation in the hazard ratio over time, particularly early in follow-up, but no strong evidence of a substantial violation of the proportional hazards assumption. This is consistent with the formal test in Question 21, which provides no statistical evidence against proportional hazards for hormone_receptor.

Note that the curves become unstable at the far right (few patients at risk), so judgement should focus on the region where the risk set is adequate.

22.

Two standard strategies:

  • Stratification. Include the variable as a stratification factor instead of a covariate, coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ x + strata(z), data = ...). This allows the baseline hazard to differ between the levels of \(z\) without estimating its effect. You do not get a hazard ratio for \(z\) itself, but you get valid, adjusted hazard ratios for the other covariates. It works best for a categorical variable with few levels and when its effect is not of primary interest.
  • A time-varying effect. Model the coefficient as a function of time, e.g. by adding an interaction between the covariate and a function of time (Collett, Section 4.4.4), or by a time-dependent coefficient model. This yields an effect estimate that changes over time, so the single “average” hazard ratio is replaced by a description of how the hazard ratio evolves.

Others: report the hazard ratio as a time-averaged effect with an explicit caveat; or switch to a model that does not require proportional hazards, such as an accelerated failure time / parametric model (Chapters 5–7) or a restricted mean survival time analysis. Whatever the choice, the important point is that a non-proportional effect should not be reported as a single constant hazard ratio without comment.
